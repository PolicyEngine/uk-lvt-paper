\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Where land value sits}
\label{sec:whereland}

The distributional consequences of an LVT are determined by the distribution of its base, which we describe first. Table~\ref{tab:land_income} reports average land value, property wealth and land shares by decile of equivalised household net income. Average land value rises with income --- from \pounds 126{,}808 in the poorest decile to \pounds 443{,}238 in the richest --- but less than proportionally: average income in decile 10 is 6.8 times that of decile 1, while its average land value is 3.5 times as large. The top two income deciles together hold 35.4 per cent of household land.

Regional variation is at least as pronounced as variation by income. Average household land value is \pounds 369{,}802 in London and \pounds 286{,}311 in the South East, compared with \pounds 153{,}631 in the North East and \pounds 159{,}535 in Scotland (Figure~\ref{fig:land_region}) --- a ratio of 2.4 to 1 that reflects both higher property prices and higher land shares of those prices in the South. Across household types, pensioner couples hold the most land on average (\pounds 431{,}377), followed by working-age couples without children (\pounds 303{,}440); lone parents, who are predominantly renters, hold very little (\pounds 7{,}797) (Figure~\ref{fig:land_famtype}). Ranked by total wealth rather than income, concentration is far greater: the bottom three wealth deciles hold essentially no land, while the top wealth decile averages \pounds 1{,}065{,}658 --- 48.5 per cent of all household land (Table~\ref{tab:wealth_impact} below). Overall, 17.2 per cent of households hold no land.

\subsection{Revenue and the rate schedule}
\label{sec:revenue}

Because the base is fixed in the static framework, LVT revenue is linear in the rate: each 0.1 percentage point raises approximately \pounds 7.5 billion. Table~\ref{tab:revenue} reports revenue across the rate grid. A 0.5 per cent rate raises \pounds 37.3 billion --- \pounds 20.3 billion less than council tax --- while a 1 per cent rate raises \pounds 74.6 billion and a 5 per cent rate raises \pounds 373.2 billion. The budget-neutral rate is 0.77 per cent (equation~\ref{eq:rate}). Decomposing the base at a 1 per cent rate, household land contributes \pounds 54.0 billion and corporate land \pounds 20.6 billion of the \pounds 74.6 billion total. The corporate component --- 28 per cent of revenue --- falls on households in proportion to their shareholdings, and its inclusion permits a lower rate for any given revenue target than a purely residential base would require.

\subsection{Distributional impact by income decile}
\label{sec:income_impact}

Table~\ref{tab:income_impact} and Figure~\ref{fig:net_income} report the central result: the average change in household net income at the budget-neutral 0.77 per cent rate, by income decile. The average household in each of deciles 1--8 gains. The poorest decile gains most in both absolute and relative terms --- \pounds 481 per year, or 2.3 per cent of net income --- because its average council tax bill (\pounds 1{,}460, reflecting the compressed banding structure) substantially exceeds the 0.77 per cent charge on its comparatively modest land holdings. Deciles 9 and 10 lose \pounds 991 and \pounds 966 per year respectively: their average land holdings of approximately \pounds 420{,}000--\pounds 443{,}000 attract LVT liabilities of \pounds 3{,}200--\pounds 3{,}400 against council tax savings of \pounds 2{,}200--\pounds 2{,}500. In percentage terms the loss is larger for decile 9 ($-1.3$ per cent) than for decile 10 ($-0.7$ per cent), because top-decile incomes are considerably higher while average land holdings are only slightly larger.

\subsection{Distributional impact by wealth decile}
\label{sec:wealth_impact}

Table~\ref{tab:wealth_impact} and Figure~\ref{fig:net_wealth} report the corresponding results when households are ranked by total wealth rather than income. The bottom wealth decile, which owns no land, retains its full council tax saving of \pounds 927 per year; wealth deciles 2--7 gain between \pounds 484 and \pounds 1{,}464 on average; decile 8 approximately breaks even ($-$\pounds 296); decile 9 loses \pounds 1{,}042; and the top wealth decile --- averaging over \pounds 1 million of land --- loses \pounds 5{,}573 per year, with an average LVT liability of \pounds 8{,}230 against council tax savings of \pounds 2{,}657, and 98.3 per cent of its households losing. The incidence of the reform thus tracks land wealth closely, consistent with the design of the tax.\footnote{Revenue neutrality holds for the aggregate tax swap --- LVT revenue equals abolished council tax revenue at \pounds 57.6 billion --- but the per-decile average net-income changes reported here need not sum to zero across deciles. They are per-household averages of the change in net income, which incorporates benefit interactions (notably council tax reduction) as well as the two tax lines, and decile group sizes differ. The population-weighted aggregate net-income change is identical whether households are grouped by income decile or by wealth decile: the two decile charts show the same set of households, merely re-sorted.}

\subsection{Winners and losers within deciles}
\label{sec:winners}

Decile averages conceal substantial within-decile variation, because land holdings vary widely within any given income band: an outright owner-occupier in London and a private renter in the North East may occupy the same income decile. At the budget-neutral rate, 68 per cent of all households gain. The share of gaining households declines only gradually with income through deciles 1--8 (from 76.5 to 68.6 per cent) before falling to just under one half in deciles 9--10 (Figure~\ref{fig:winners}). Even in the top income decile, 48.6 per cent of households gain --- predominantly high earners who rent or own property of modest value --- while approximately one in five bottom-decile households loses, typically low-income owners of valuable land. Sorted by wealth, the gradient is considerably steeper: the share of gaining households exceeds 80 per cent in each of the bottom seven wealth deciles and falls to 1.7 per cent in the top decile. The 17.2 per cent of households holding no land gain \pounds 832 per year on average, and 81.5 per cent of them gain; the remainder are largely households whose council tax was already fully offset by council tax reduction.

\subsection{Poverty and inequality}
\label{sec:poverty}

Table~\ref{tab:poverty} reports poverty and inequality across the rate grid, measured against baseline-fixed absolute poverty thresholds. At the budget-neutral 0.77 per cent rate, absolute BHC poverty falls from 12.47 to 11.82 per cent ($-0.65$ percentage points) and AHC poverty from 15.48 to 14.43 per cent ($-1.04$ points): for many low-income households, council tax savings exceed LVT liabilities, moving some above the poverty threshold. The Gini coefficient of equivalised household net income is essentially unchanged, rising by 0.0004 from its baseline of 0.366. The tax burden moves sharply up the wealth distribution (Section~\ref{sec:wealth_impact}), but because land wealth correlates imperfectly with income, losses in income deciles 9--10 are approximately offset, in Gini terms, by losses among asset-rich households located in lower income deciles.

The grid indicates that these results are sensitive to the rate. At 0.5 per cent, which reduces net revenue by \pounds 20.3 billion, poverty falls further (BHC $-0.89$, AHC $-1.42$ points) and the Gini declines slightly ($-0.0028$). At 1 per cent, which raises \pounds 17 billion of net revenue, BHC poverty is marginally above baseline (+0.22 points) while AHC poverty remains below it. At higher rates, poverty and measured income inequality rise substantially --- at 5 per cent, BHC poverty rises by 11.5 points and the Gini by 0.10 --- because the static analysis levies cash charges on asset-rich, income-poor households with no offsetting use of the additional revenue. These upper-grid figures should be interpreted as the gross incidence of the tax alone rather than as complete reform packages: any practical proposal at higher rates would recycle the net revenue. The wealth Gini (0.7038) is mechanically unchanged, as the simulation does not model asset-price responses.
