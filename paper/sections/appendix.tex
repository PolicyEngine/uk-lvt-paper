\subsection{Landless households}

Households holding no land --- neither residential property nor corporate wealth --- make up 17.2 per cent of the population. Because their LVT liability is zero at any rate, their outcome is invariant across the rate grid: an average gain of \pounds 832 per year (their abolished council tax bill), with 81.0 per cent winning. The non-winners are predominantly households whose council tax was already fully covered by council tax reduction, for whom abolition changes nothing.

\subsection{Worked single-household example}

Consider a household owning a \pounds 400{,}000 home in London, where the land share is 85 per cent, implying household land of \pounds 340{,}000. If the household also holds \pounds 50{,}000 of corporate wealth, its attributed corporate land is that sum multiplied by the population ratio of aggregate corporate land to aggregate corporate wealth, which the simulation puts at \pounds 0.145 per pound of corporate wealth, or \pounds 7{,}257. Its total land value is therefore \pounds 347{,}257 and its annual liability at the budget-neutral rate (0.772 per cent before rounding) is \pounds 2{,}682; at a 1 per cent rate it would be \pounds 3{,}473. Whether the household gains depends on the abolished council tax bill: with a typical upper-band London bill of around \pounds 2{,}400 it loses roughly \pounds 280 a year, and it breaks even at a bill of \pounds 2{,}682.

The allocation ratio must be computed against the weighted population. Evaluating equation~(\ref{eq:land}) inside a single-household simulation assigns the entire \pounds 2.06 trillion corporate land base to that one record, because it constitutes the whole population; the example above therefore uses the population ratio from the microsimulation.

\subsection{Deferral for pensioner households}

The liquidity problem the reform creates is concentrated and measurable. Households containing at least one person of state pension age make up 27.2 per cent of the population and would owe \pounds 20.3 billion of the \pounds 57.6 billion total, or 35.1 per cent of the yield, at an average liability of \pounds 2{,}318. A full deferral option for pensioner households --- the liability rolled up with interest against the property, as recommended by \citet{mirrlees2011} --- would therefore postpone rather than forgo roughly a third of receipts in the first year, declining thereafter as deferred estates are settled. This is the scale of the transitional financing problem; it is large but bounded, and it is a cash-flow cost to government rather than a revenue loss.

\subsection{Replication note}

All results are generated from a single baseline simulation of PolicyEngine UK 2.88.20 on the pinned Enhanced FRS release \texttt{enhanced\_frs\_2023\_24@1.55.10}, together with the closed-form arithmetic validated in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}. The generating script (\texttt{uk\_lvt/pipeline\_direct.py}) re-runs the full model at three rates on every invocation and refuses to write results if the arithmetic and the model disagree by more than \pounds 1 per household or 0.01 percentage points of poverty.

Three caveats on measurement and version sensitivity are worth recording. First, all poverty rates are shares of individuals (household poverty status weighted by household size, the HBAI convention) and the income Gini is person-weighted over equivalised HBAI net income; household-weighted, unequivalised versions of the same statistics --- which earlier drafts of this paper reported --- give higher levels (baseline BHC poverty of 11.53 rather than 9.85 per cent, a Gini of 0.364 rather than 0.298) but the same qualitative results. Second, poverty \emph{levels} move with the underlying benefit and uprating parameters across model releases: under release 2.88.10 the estimated BHC change at the budget-neutral rate was negative rather than the $+0.03$ points reported here. The revenue, land and winner-share results are unaffected, but the sign of the small BHC poverty effect is not robust across model versions, which is why we do not claim a poverty reduction on that measure. Third, the wealth-decile results depend on how deciles are constructed in the presence of tied zero-wealth households; we construct equal-weight deciles directly rather than using the model's own decile variable.
