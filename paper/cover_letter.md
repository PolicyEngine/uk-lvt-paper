# Cover letter — Fiscal Studies submission

Dear Editors,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, "Replacing council tax with a land
value tax: a household-level microsimulation for the UK," for consideration at
*Fiscal Studies*.

The paper develops an open-source household-level framework for simulating
land value tax reform in the United Kingdom and applies it to a family of
scenarios in which council tax is abolished and replaced by a flat annual tax
on land value. Land values are imputed to every household in the Enhanced
Family Resources Survey — combining survey property wealth, region-specific
land shares derived from MHCLG land value estimates, and an allocation of
corporate-sector land — and benchmarked against ONS National Balance Sheet
aggregates. The scenarios span a grid of rates from 0.5 to 5 per cent,
revenue recycling as a citizen's dividend, exempt bands of land value, and
packages that also replace stamp duty, anchored by a budget-neutral reference
scenario at 0.79 per cent.

Three features may interest your readers. First, the paper's central
analytical result is a caution about measurement: the reform's incidence is
organised by wealth rather than income, so the income-based statistics that
dominate UK distributional debate register almost nothing — our bootstrap
inference shows the changes in before-housing-cost poverty and the Gini
coefficient are statistically indistinguishable from zero — while against the
wealth distribution the reform is large, monotonic, and precisely estimated:
council tax's wealth-ranked Kakwani index of −0.523 becomes −0.032. A
cross-tabulation of mean effects by income decile against wealth decile makes
the dissociation directly visible. Second, the design options usually
discussed as concessions turn out not to be: an exempt band of land value
makes the reform more progressive, and recycling the proceeds of a higher
rate as a per-person dividend reduces poverty on both measures. Third, the
analysis is topical: we benchmark the government's High Value Council Tax
Surcharge, announced in the 2025 Budget, against the reform family on the
same microdata.

We treat the key incidence assumptions as sensitivity dimensions rather than
maintained hypotheses, reporting results as a function of the rent
pass-through share from zero to one (engaging with the recent German
evidence) alongside capitalisation bounds, alternative bases and replacement
targets, a proportional property tax comparator, and a Monte Carlo exercise
on within-region land shares. The framework sits in the tax-benefit
microsimulation tradition of EUROMOD; the closest methodological precedent is
Advani, Hughson and Tarrant's wealth tax analysis in this journal. All code
is open source and the results are reproducible from a pinned data release.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. Both authors have
approved the submission and declare no competing interests.

Thank you for your consideration.
