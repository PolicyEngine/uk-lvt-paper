\begin{abstract}
\noindent Council tax, the UK's principal recurrent property tax, rests on 1991 valuations and is regressive with respect to property value. We use PolicyEngine UK, an open-source static microsimulation model built on the Enhanced Family Resources Survey, to simulate replacing council tax's \pounds 57.6 billion of net revenue with a flat annual tax on the unimproved value of land, imputing land to every household from Wealth and Assets Survey property wealth, region-specific land shares derived from MHCLG land value estimates, and an allocation of corporate land. Against a land base of \pounds 7.46 trillion the budget-neutral rate is 0.77 per cent. We find that 68 per cent of households gain and that absolute poverty after housing costs falls by 0.70 percentage points, while poverty before housing costs and the Gini coefficient of equivalised net income are both essentially unchanged. The central result is a dissociation between the two ways of ranking households. Across income deciles the reform is close to distributionally flat, with average effects in deciles 2--8 of a few hundred pounds either way and clear losses confined to the top two deciles. Across wealth deciles the gradient is monotonic and steep: the bottom seven gain, and the top decile --- which holds 47 per cent of all land --- loses \pounds 5{,}752 a year, with 98 per cent of its households worse off. Because land is highly concentrated by wealth but only weakly correlated with current income, income-based distributional statistics are close to uninformative about this reform. The pattern survives changes to the replacement target, the treatment of corporate and foreign-owned land, the geographic scope and plausible perturbations of the land shares; full capitalisation would imply a one-off fall in land values of 15--26 per cent, borne overwhelmingly by the top wealth decile.
\end{abstract}
