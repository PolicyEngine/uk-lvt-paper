\begin{abstract}
\noindent Council tax, the UK's principal recurrent property tax, rests on 1991 valuations: it takes 1.8 per cent of property value on the cheapest homes and 0.1 per cent on the most expensive. Using PolicyEngine UK on the Enhanced Family Resources Survey, we simulate replacing its \pounds 57.6 billion of net revenue with a flat annual tax on land, imputed to every household and benchmarked against ONS balance-sheet aggregates. The budget-neutral rate is 0.77 per cent. Sixty-eight per cent of households gain and after-housing-cost poverty falls by 0.70 percentage points, while measured income inequality is unchanged. Incidence is organised by wealth, not income: against the wealth ranking, council tax has a Kakwani index of $-0.518$ and the land tax $-0.032$, close to proportional, and the top wealth decile loses \pounds 5{,}752 a year; against the income ranking the two are barely distinguishable. Income-based distributional statistics are therefore nearly uninformative about this reform.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
