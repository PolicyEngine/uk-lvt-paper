\begin{abstract}
\noindent Council tax, the UK's principal recurrent property tax, rests on 1991 valuations and takes 1.8 per cent of property value from the cheapest homes but 0.1 per cent from the most expensive. Using PolicyEngine UK on the Enhanced Family Resources Survey, we simulate replacing its \pounds 57.6 billion of net revenue with a flat annual tax on land, imputed to every household and benchmarked against ONS aggregates. The revenue-neutral rate is 0.77 per cent. Two thirds of households gain, poverty after housing costs falls 0.70 points, and measured income inequality is unchanged. Incidence is organised by wealth, not income: ranked by wealth, council tax has a Kakwani index of $-0.518$ against the land tax's $-0.032$, near proportionality, and the richest decile loses \pounds 5{,}752 a year; ranked by income the two are barely distinguishable. Conventional income-based statistics are therefore nearly uninformative here.
\end{abstract}
